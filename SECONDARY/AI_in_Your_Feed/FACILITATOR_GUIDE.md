# Facilitator Guide

## Teaching stance

Teach this course with curiosity, steadiness, and backbone.

Do not teach AI as magic.
Do not teach AI as neutral.
Do not teach AI as the enemy either.

Teach it as a set of systems made by people, funded by institutions, trained on culture, and deployed unevenly.

## North Minneapolis teaching notes

This course is built for a place with deep creativity, deep cultural memory, and very real experience with unequal systems.

That means:
- avoid deficit framing
- avoid asking students to disclose harm for the sake of discussion
- do not pressure students to use personal photos, names, voices, addresses, or private stories
- let students choose whether their work is local, personal, fictional, or abstract
- center dignity, authorship, and consent
- assume students already have important insights about feeds, visibility, trends, and misrepresentation

## Community agreements to establish in Session 1

Suggested agreements:
1. We protect each other’s privacy.
2. We do not upload personal information.
3. We can disagree with a tool and with each other respectfully.
4. We credit sources when we know them.
5. We do not use AI to humiliate, impersonate, or target people.
6. We make room for revision; first outputs are rarely final outputs.
7. We do not let the tool do all the thinking for us.

## Room setup

Best arrangement:
- small clusters or pods of 2–4 students
- projector or large display for demos
- visible board or wall for key terms
- headphones if audio tools are used
- printed handouts for privacy and bias-check routines

## Recommended pacing for 90-minute sessions

A reliable rhythm:
- 10–15 min opening / share / frame
- 15–20 min demo or discussion
- 35–45 min studio time
- 10–15 min reflection / share-out
- last 5 min exit note and cleanup

## Tool selection principles

Choose tools that are:
- age-appropriate
- easy to access
- low-friction for login
- transparent enough to discuss
- safe enough for school/community use

Prefer:
- school accounts over personal accounts
- tools that allow anonymous or minimal-data use
- classroom-visible tools over black-box novelty apps
- tools that produce exportable work students can discuss later

## Safety defaults

Before students use any tool, say this out loud:

- Do not upload your face, your full name, your address, your phone number, your school ID, or private family information.
- Do not upload other people’s faces or voices without consent.
- Do not connect random apps to your personal accounts.
- Ask before sharing work publicly.
- If a tool asks for more than it needs, pause and talk.

## Facilitation moves that work well

When a tool gives a weak output, ask:
- What is missing?
- What feels generic?
- What feels stereotyped?
- What details changed the result?
- What would make this more like your voice?

When a tool gives a suspicious factual answer, ask:
- How would we verify that?
- What source would count as trustworthy here?
- What part of the answer sounds confident without proof?

When students are excited by the speed of a tool, ask:
- What part did you decide?
- What part did the model decide?
- What did you keep?
- What did you reject?

## Materials checklist

- laptops or desktops
- internet access if cloud tools are used
- projector
- sticky notes or index cards
- markers
- headphones
- printed handouts from `HANDOUTS/`
- optional: notebooks for prompt logs and reflection

## Low-tech fallback plan

If the internet is unstable or account setup fails:
- turn the day into analysis and design
- use screenshots of outputs instead of live tools
- have students write prompts on paper and predict likely outputs
- compare multiple sample outputs
- build storyboards, level ideas, or project plans offline
- close with a tool-safety discussion and a paper prototype

## Suggested assessment approach

Use feedback-based assessment, not high-pressure grading.

Look for:
- effort and revision
- critical awareness
- creative decision-making
- safe use habits
- reflection quality
- clarity about what AI did and did not do

## Extension pathways

After the 4-session set, this course can branch into:
- AI and game design
- AI image, video, and motion
- AI sound and music
- AI, jobs, and entrepreneurship
- AI bias, civics, and community research
- local-first / small-model workflows
