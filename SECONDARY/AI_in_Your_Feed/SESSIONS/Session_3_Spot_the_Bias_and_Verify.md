# Session 3 — Spot the Bias and Verify

## Theme
Critical thinking and ethics

## Session purpose
Students learn to look harder at AI output. The point is not to turn them cynical; it is to sharpen their eyes. They should leave understanding that bias, misinformation, and flattening are not side issues. They are part of the medium.

## Learning objectives
Students will:
- identify common forms of AI bias and factual error
- practice checking outputs against other sources or lived reality
- revise prompts to reduce flattening and stereotype
- reflect on ethical use

## Essential question
How do I know when an AI output is misleading, biased, or just plain wrong?

## Materials
- projector
- sample outputs prepared in advance
- student projects from Session 2
- `HANDOUTS/AI_Survival_and_Creation_Guide.md`
- optional “bias check” worksheet or notebook page

## Prep
Prepare 3–5 examples of problematic outputs:
- an inaccurate factual summary
- a stereotyped visual description
- an overconfident made-up answer
- a suspicious citation list
- a flattened neighborhood or community description

## Agenda

### 1. Discussion — 15 min
Start with examples from media, feeds, search, and generative tools.

Prompt:
- Have you ever seen something online that looked polished but felt wrong?
- What makes a generated answer sound believable?
- What gets lost when a system smooths everything into the same voice?

### 2. Interactive exercise — 20 min
In pairs or small groups, students inspect prepared outputs.

Ask them to mark:
- what feels inaccurate
- what feels stereotyped
- what would need verification
- what details feel missing
- where the tone hides uncertainty

### 3. Studio time — 40 min
Students return to their projects and do one of the following:
- test multiple prompts for bias
- revise a visual prompt to avoid stereotype
- fact-check a text-heavy output
- annotate what changed and why
- create a version that is more fair, accurate, or specific

### 4. Reflection and wrap-up — 15 min
Discussion prompts:
- What patterns did you notice?
- When does AI flatten people or places?
- How do you protect your own voice from becoming generic?
- What kind of project should always be fact-checked?

## Output
Students leave with:
- one annotated example of bias or error
- one revised project element
- one strategy for verification they can explain

## Homework / practice
Create one small AI output and annotate it:
- Where might bias show up?
- What did you verify?
- What source or lived knowledge helped you judge it?

## North Minneapolis note
This session can be especially strong if it examines how neighborhoods, race, style, language, and public perception get compressed into clichés. Keep discussion focused on systems and representations rather than asking students to disclose personal harm unless they choose to.
