## Course description

In this course, students learn to see the digital image as both a technical structure and a living language.

We will move between cameras and screens; between pixels, layers, and color spaces; between careful repair and joyful glitch. Students will practice capturing images, editing and compositing them, and building short series that say something about their world.

The course culminates in a small exhibition or digital gallery with written artist statements.

## Learning goals

By the end of this course, students will be able to:

1. **Explain how digital images work**
   - Describe pixels, resolution, and basic file formats (JPG, PNG).
   - Recognize the impact of compression and resizing on image quality.

2. **Capture images with intention**
   - Apply basic composition techniques (rule of thirds, leading lines, framing).
   - Make choices about light, viewpoint, and timing to support an idea.

3. **Edit images using a layer-based editor**
   - Crop, straighten, and adjust tone (brightness/contrast, levels).
   - Adjust color and use simple color grading to change mood.
   - Use layers, selections, and masks to create simple collages or composites.

4. **Create short image series**
   - Develop 2–5 related images that explore a theme, place, or feeling.
   - Write brief artist statements connecting technical choices to intent.

5. **Engage with image ethics**
   - Discuss consent and representation in photography.
   - Identify when an edited image could be misleading or harmful.
   - Reflect on AI-generated imagery as part of the contemporary image ecosystem.

6. **Experiment with images as code**
   - Use a simple p5.js (or similar) sketch to manipulate or generate images.
   - Understand that images can be transformed algorithmically, not just by hand.

## Audience & prerequisites

- **Intended audience:** Students in grades 6–9.
- **Prerequisites:**  
  - Comfort using a computer (file saving, basic navigation).  
  - No prior experience with photography or editing required.

Mixed-level groups are expected; the course is designed with extensions for students who move quickly and scaffolds for students who are newer to digital tools.

## Time & structure

- **Sessions:** 12
- **Length:** 90 minutes each
- **Structure:** Most sessions follow an A/B block:
  - **Block A (≈40–45 min):** Warm-up + mini-lesson + guided practice.
  - **Block B (≈40–45 min):** Studio time on individual or group projects, check-ins, and short reflections.

## Major projects

1. **Pixel Self-Portrait (Sessions 2–4)**
   - Students create a low-resolution self-portrait image (photo-based or pixel art) and upscale it intentionally, learning about resolution, pixelation, and file formats.

2. **Poster Campaign (Sessions 5–7)**
   - Students design a poster that communicates a message (school event, social issue, fictional band, etc.) combining photograph(s), text, and graphic elements.

3. **Experimental Series (Sessions 8–12)**
   - Students create 3–5 images using glitch, retouch, color grading, or generative/code-based transformations to explore a theme. This becomes the core of the final exhibition.

See `projects/` for detailed prompts and teacher notes.

## Materials & software

- **Hardware**
  - 1 computer per 1–2 students.
  - Cameras: phones/tablets or point-and-shoots (enough for small groups).
  - Optional: Printer for physical exhibition.

- **Software**
  - A **layer-based image editor** such as:
    - Photopea (browser-based),
    - GIMP,
    - Photoshop if available.
  - Web browser with access to p5.js editor or an equivalent coding environment.

See `resources/software-options.md` for more detailed tool suggestions and configuration notes.

## Assessment

- **In-class work (process) — 40%**
  - Engagement in exercises and studio work.
  - Willingness to experiment and revise.

- **Projects (products) — 40%**
  - Pixel Self-Portrait
  - Poster Campaign
  - Experimental Series (final)

- **Reflection & critique — 20%**
  - Artist statements.
  - Participation in peer critique with constructive feedback.

Rubrics live in `rubrics/` and are meant to be adapted by the instructor.

## Classroom norms

- We **ask for consent** before photographing people.
- We **credit sources** for images we did not create.
- We **respect each other’s boundaries**—no shaming, no posting others’ images without discussion.
- We aim for **playful rigor**: try wild ideas, but back them up with craft.

## Accessibility & differentiation

- Provide image examples at different difficulty levels.
- Offer both **step-by-step recipes** and **open prompts**.
- Allow students to work with:
  - Found images (with clear rights/attribution),
  - Their own photographs,
  - Generated images (with an ethics conversation).

Students with strong drawing skills can integrate hand-drawn art via scanning or photographing; students without those skills can focus on composition, color, and collage.
