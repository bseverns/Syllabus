### Assignment: Gesture Tester – Shake & Tilt

**Title:** `Gesture Tester – Shake & Tilt`

**Instructions:**

> You’ll make the micro:bit react differently when you shake it or tilt it.  
>  
> 1. Open your previous project or start a new MakeCode project called `GestureTester`.  
> 2. Add code so that:  
>    • When you **shake** the micro:bit, it shows a random icon or number.  
>    • When you **tilt left**, it shows a left arrow.  
>    • When you **tilt right**, it shows a right arrow.  
> 3. Test all three gestures.  
> 4. Turn in:  
>    • Screenshot/photo of your code  
>    • A 1–2 sentence explanation:  
>      > “The accelerometer is a sensor that notices ______. I used it so that when I ______, the micro:bit ______.”

### Assignment: Reaction Timer – Build or Modify

**Title:** `Reaction Timer – Build or Modify`

**Instructions:**

> We’re turning the micro:bit into a simple reaction game.  
>  
> 1. On paper (or in the attached doc), write an algorithm (step-by-step list) for this game:  
>    • On start, wait a random amount of time.  
>    • Then turn on the LED grid as a signal.  
>    • When the player presses button A, show how fast they reacted.  
> 2. In MakeCode, either:  
>    • Build this program using blocks, OR  
>    • Open the sample project you are given and modify it.  
> 3. Test:  
>    • Does the signal appear after a random delay?  
>    • Does pressing A show a “score” or time?  
> 4. Turn in:  
>    • Photo/screenshot of your code  
>    • Your written algorithm (photo or doc)  
>    • A sentence:  
>      > “One difference between my algorithm on paper and my code is ______.”

---

### Assignment: Controller Prototype – Cardboard Build

**Title:** `Controller Prototype – Cardboard Build`

**Instructions:**

> You’ll design and build a simple physical shell for your micro:bit so it feels like a small handheld controller.  
>  
> 1. Sketch your design on paper:  
>    • Where will the micro:bit sit?  
>    • Where will the player’s hands be?  
>    • How will they reach buttons A and B?  
> 2. Using cardboard and tape, build a first version of your housing.  
> 3. Place your micro:bit (running any of your programs) into the housing and test holding it.  
> 4. Ask a partner to hold and use it for 1 minute. Observe what’s comfortable or awkward.  
> 5. Turn in:  
>    • A photo of your controller prototype with the micro:bit in it  
>    • A few bullet points:  
>      > What works well: ______  
>      > What feels awkward: ______  
>      > One change you want to try next: ______

### Material: Debugging Checklist

**Title:** `Debugging Checklist`

**Description:**

> A simple checklist for troubleshooting:  
> • Is the board powered and connected?  
> • Does the program run as expected?  
> • What input should cause what output?  
>  
> We’ll use this to debug both code and hardware.

---

### Assignment: Debug & Refine – Make It Reliable

**Title:** `Debug & Refine – Make It Reliable`

**Instructions:**

> Today is about fixing bugs and improving comfort.  
>  
> 1. Run your best micro:bit program (MoodFace, GestureTester, or ReactionTimer).  
> 2. Use the debugging checklist to look for problems, such as:  
>    • Input doesn’t always trigger output.  
>    • The game restarts unexpectedly.  
>    • The controller housing blocks buttons or LEDs.  
> 3. Fix at least:  
>    • One bug in your code **or**  
>    • One issue in your physical design (tape, shape, labels).  
> 4. Retest with a partner.  
> 5. Turn in:  
>    • A short note (3–4 sentences) describing:  
>      > The bug or issue you found  
>      > What you changed  
>      > If it solved the problem or what still feels off
