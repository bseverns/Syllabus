# Instructor Guide

## Teaching stance

Teach debugging as a form of inquiry, not a cleanup chore.

Students should hear repeatedly that:

- bugs are information
- confusion can be studied
- fast answers are not always the best answers
- asking for help is part of strong debugging, not proof of failure

## Best fit for this version

This secondary version works well when students already have some experience building things in code, media, or physical systems, but do not need to be advanced programmers.

Good classrooms for this course:

- intro CS
- creative coding
- robotics
- game design
- digital media
- maker or STEM electives

## Recommended materials

- one shared platform for building or testing
- projector for live debugging demos
- printed handouts or digital copies of the debugging log and bug report
- timer for short debugging rounds

## What to emphasize

- evidence before theory
- one change at a time
- multiple legitimate goals
- emotional honesty without making the class therapeutic
- reflection that names process, not just success

## What usually goes wrong

- students jump to fixing before describing the bug
- students change too many things at once
- peer support becomes takeover instead of coaching
- teachers reward only speed and correctness
- refactoring gets taught as morally superior instead of context-dependent

## Good teacher moves

- ask "what do we know for sure?"
- ask "what changed right before this happened?"
- ask "what are you trying to optimize right now?"
- ask "what is one small test you can run next?"
- ask "what emotion is in the room, and how is it affecting the next move?"

## Assessment posture

Grade debugging as a visible practice:

- clarity
- evidence
- iteration
- communication
- reflection

Do not grade only whether the final bug disappeared.
