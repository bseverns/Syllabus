# Ethics, Accessibility, and Long-Term Thinking — Facilitator Launch Guide

Use this with the syllabus, schedule, weekly sessions, templates, and grading rubric. The course is intentionally platform-flexible: students may improve hardware, software, installations, documentation, services, or an instructor-supplied case-study artifact.

## Launch boundary

- 12 weeks for advanced high-school or undergraduate learners
- one 150–180 minute studio or two 90-minute meetings weekly
- no paid tool, personal device, public repository, or custom hardware required
- one bounded artifact per student/team, selected by the end of Week 1
- one small tested improvement plus a complete Responsibility Pack

Students do not need prior engineering or accessibility vocabulary. They do need a real or simulated artifact they can inspect and permission to modify either the artifact itself or its documentation/prototype copy.

## Minimum materials for 16 students

- paper or private digital folders for each student/team;
- printed or accessible digital copies of all templates;
- sticky notes, index cards, markers, tape, label tape, tactile dots, rulers, and contrast samples;
- simple removable knobs, grips, cardboard/foam mockup material, and fasteners;
- timer, projector/large paper, and a quiet/private feedback area;
- private evidence storage with a defined retention/deletion plan;
- 4–6 instructor-supplied case-study artifacts with photographs, manuals, user journeys, and known constraints.

Optional by project: basic hand tools, replacement controls, small enclosure parts, screen reader/keyboard testing access, caption/transcript tools, audio meters, or the host lab's approved prototyping equipment.

Do not make students purchase an artifact or disclose disability/access information to participate.

## Artifact-selection fallback

An acceptable artifact is small enough to examine in 12 weeks and has a specific user action. Examples: a classroom device, open-source tool, website flow, controller, instrument, kiosk mockup, instruction sheet, booking form, or instructor case packet.

Reject or rescope artifacts when students lack permission, the system handles high-risk personal data, the change requires professional certification, the affected community is unavailable and speculation would be harmful, or the project promises universal accessibility.

Every team records:

- artifact owner/steward and allowed scope;
- intended users without inventing a single “normal user”;
- what may be changed, documented, tested, or published;
- data collected, storage location, retention, and deletion date;
- safe fallback if real-user testing is not appropriate.

## Before Week 1

- Choose 4–6 case-study artifacts and complete a sample Promise and Harm Map for one.
- Test that every template opens, saves, prints, and works with the local accessibility tools.
- Define private submission and optional publication routes.
- Publish consent, recording, critique, and withdrawal expectations.
- Identify which modifications require lab staff or artifact-owner approval.
- Prepare asynchronous, nonverbal, and no-personal-disclosure critique routes.
- Decide how students request access support without reporting it to peers.

## Default 165-minute rhythm

| Time | Move |
| --- | --- |
| 0–15 min | Access/consent check, concrete case, and visible question |
| 15–35 min | Plain-language concept framing and model artifact |
| 35–60 min | Individual/team inspection using the week's template |
| 60–70 min | Break and sensory reset |
| 70–110 min | Audit, documentation, or redesign sprint |
| 110–130 min | Protocol-based peer test or critique |
| 130–150 min | Revise one claim/change using evidence |
| 150–165 min | Save privately, record unknowns, cleanup, and next action |

## Plain-language opening

Say this:

> You are not expected to arrive knowing accessibility law, design vocabulary, or a particular technology. We will make specific promises, look for evidence, test small improvements, and document what remains unknown. You never need to disclose a diagnosis or personal access need to make a strong design argument.

Shared words:

- **artifact:** the tool, system, document, or service being studied;
- **stakeholder:** someone affected by decisions about the artifact;
- **barrier:** a condition that blocks or increases the cost of participation;
- **mitigation:** a change that reduces a specific risk or barrier;
- **consent:** informed, specific, voluntary agreement that can be withdrawn;
- **accessibility:** designing participation so more people can perceive, understand, operate, and recover;
- **repairability:** how possible it is to diagnose, maintain, and restore a system;
- **lifecycle:** what happens from materials and creation through use, repair, reuse, and disposal;
- **known unknown:** an uncertainty documented rather than hidden.

## Testing without extraction

- Never require a student or guest to disclose disability, diagnosis, or trauma.
- Do not ask one disabled person to represent a whole community or provide unpaid expert certification.
- Test specific tasks and observable barriers, not a person's body or worthiness.
- Offer compensation when inviting external expertise under local policy.
- Use expert guidance, published standards, protocol checks, simulations, and instructor case data when direct testing is inappropriate.
- Obtain separate permission for participation, recording, quotation, and publication.
- Let participants stop, skip questions, or withdraw material without penalty.

## Participation and access routes

- Accept paper, audio, video, diagram, prototype, code, or documentation contributions when the same reasoning is evident.
- Provide documents in structured, selectable-text formats and describe images/diagrams.
- Use keyboard and nonvisual checks where relevant; never treat one automated score as accessibility proof.
- Make critique possible verbally, in writing, asynchronously, or through a facilitator.
- Grade evidence and improvement—not polish, disclosure, expensive fabrication, or public contribution.
- An internal documentation upgrade can meet the ecosystem-contribution goal when upstream publication is unsafe or unwanted.

## Common-stuck and recovery guide

| What happens | Next move |
| --- | --- |
| Student has no artifact | Assign an instructor case packet; do not delay the course while searching for a “perfect” project |
| Claim becomes “accessible for everyone” | Name the specific task, context, users considered, evidence, exclusions, and unknowns |
| Audit becomes a fault list | Select one barrier, trace its cause, and design the smallest meaningful mitigation |
| No direct user test is appropriate | Use a protocol walkthrough, assistive-technology check, expert source, role-neutral task test, or documented simulation |
| Team wants sensitive personal data | Remove it unless strictly necessary; use fabricated/redacted data and document the decision |
| Hardware/software cannot be changed | Improve the interface mockup, setup guide, labeling, recovery path, maintenance plan, or procurement recommendation |
| Ethics discussion becomes abstract | Return to an owner, decision, affected person, failure mode, evidence, and next action |
| External contribution is rejected | Preserve the proposal, feedback, revision, and internal documentation improvement as evidence |

## Evidence and completion

The final Responsibility Pack includes a measurable promise, harm/barrier map, consent/data statement, accessibility audit and tested improvement, repairability/maintenance and lifecycle plan, licensing/credits, quick start, troubleshooting, contribution record, test notes, known unknowns, and postmortem.

A checklist is not certification. Students must describe the scope of evidence and what remains untested. After delivery, record artifact bottlenecks, consent/privacy issues, materials used, access routes selected, timing changes, and revisions needed before relaunch.
