# Swarm Aesthetics: Instructor Notes

## Teaching stance

Teach this as a systems-and-aesthetics studio, not a gadget showcase. The course works best when students are pushed to name the actual layers of a collective event:

- inputs
- rules
- roles
- interfaces
- audience framing
- failure points

## Recommended cohort structure

- groups of 3 to 5 work well
- form groups by complementary strengths, not just friend clusters
- make role rotation explicit early so one student does not become the invisible operator for the whole term

## Risk tiers

Choose a tier before the course starts and tell students clearly:

### Tier 1: simulation-only

- screen-based swarms
- browser or projection studies
- simple rules, mappings, and audience framing

### Tier 2: tabletop / room-scale installation

- multi-screen, tabletop, or object-based setups
- no live flight required
- more emphasis on interfaces, cueing, and staging

### Tier 3: hybrid technical stack

- sensing, telemetry, or adjacent hardware integrated into the event
- only advisable if the instructor already has a stable setup and clear safety rules

## Non-negotiables

- every group must keep readable artifacts
- every critique must cite evidence from a prototype, reading, or event
- every public-facing gesture must have an operator and fallback plan
- students should be graded on clarity and process, not just final polish

## Weekly pressure points

- Weeks 1 to 3: keep students from becoming abstract too early
- Weeks 4 to 6: keep the prototype small enough to finish
- Weeks 7 to 9: require actual run sheets and signage, not intentions
- Weeks 10 to 12: make documentation a core deliverable, not cleanup

## What usually goes wrong

- students confuse complexity with emergence
- groups skip role clarity until rehearsal, then panic
- prototypes produce interesting behavior, but no one can explain the rules that made it happen
- signage and scripts arrive too late, so the audience cannot read the event
- documentation only starts after the showing, when key evidence is already lost

## Good instructor moves

- ask "what is the smallest version that proves the idea?"
- ask "what changes if this signal disappears?"
- ask "who knows what to do when something fails?"
- ask "what will a stranger understand in the first 20 seconds?"

## Archiving

Require consistent naming and folder structure for:

- diagrams
- recipes
- scripts
- photos and clips
- revision logs
- final statements

If archiving is left vague, the final critique gets weaker fast.
