## Pacing guidance

- The course works best when students **make something public by Week 2 or 3**.
- Do not let Week 1 become a giant reading seminar. Get students out into the hallway, sidewalk, cafeteria, bus stop, library desk, or campus website on day one.
- Weeks 4-6 are the control center. If teams are still vague by the end of Week 6, reduce ambition immediately.
- Reserve real class time for documentation. If you do not, students will treat it as an afterthought and the course archive will collapse.

## Facilitation stance

- Ask "What is the claim?" before asking whether you like the artifact.
- Ask "Who is this for in the next ten days?" before entertaining scale fantasies.
- Hold students to **evidence discipline**:
  - what did they observe?
  - who did they talk to?
  - what changed after testing?
- Push teams to choose a medium for a reason, not because it looks activist-coded.

## Partner management notes

- Use partners as collaborators, not unpaid clients and not mascots.
- Make the course promise small:
  - a prototype
  - a pilot
  - a documentation bundle
  - a tested intervention concept
- Never promise permanent deployment, policy change, ongoing maintenance, or communications labor after the semester unless somebody beyond the course owns it.
- Require one partner-facing checkpoint before major production:
  - quick meeting
  - annotated email exchange
  - on-site walk
  - recorded voice memo approval

## Safety / reputational / legal caution zones

Watch closely when projects involve:

- public accusation or naming of individuals
- data collection, surveillance, or geolocation
- minors, vulnerable communities, or trauma narratives
- medical, legal, immigration, or housing guidance
- posting material in semiprivate spaces without permission
- campus branding, logos, or institutional impersonation
- amplified sound, projection, or street-facing installations

When those issues appear:

- narrow the claim
- reduce data collection
- require advisor or partner signoff
- shift from live deployment to staged prototype if needed

## Low-tech and high-tech implementation tiers

### Low-tech tier

Works well with paper, phones, laptops, and a printer:

- posters, zines, flyers, stickers
- public bulletin systems
- paper-based kiosks or feedback stations
- QR code pathways to forms or lightweight sites
- tabletop exhibits, map walls, and portable signage

### Mid-tech tier

- no-code web pages or surveys
- slide loops for lobby screens
- short-form video or motion graphics
- SMS or email information flows
- audio guides or portable listening stations

### Higher-tech tier

Only when the team can actually maintain it:

- sensor-triggered installations
- public interfaces on tablets or microcontrollers
- lightweight projection or screen systems
- small data displays with live or semi-live updates

## Failure modes and how to keep the course resilient

### Failure mode: projects become huge and cloudy
- Fix: force a one-sentence public claim and a one-week runnable version.

### Failure mode: partner goes quiet
- Fix: shift to constituency-based testing and document the shift honestly.

### Failure mode: students confuse aesthetics with impact
- Fix: ask for evidence of comprehension, use, response, or changed behavior.

### Failure mode: documentation gets postponed
- Fix: make documentation a required in-class artifact every week.

### Failure mode: teams choose tools beyond their capacity
- Fix: move them to a lower-tech version without shame.

### Failure mode: the work starts sounding extractive
- Fix: revisit consent, reciprocity, and what the class can responsibly promise.

## Practical teaching moves

- Keep a visible wall or shared board listing:
  - issue
  - audience
  - intervention
  - risk
  - next test
- Run critiques around actual artifacts on tables or screens.
- Keep a "smaller is better than vague" note visible all semester.
- Ask every team to identify:
  - what must work live
  - what can be simulated
  - what can be printed if the network dies
