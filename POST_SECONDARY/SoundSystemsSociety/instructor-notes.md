# Instructor Notes — Sound / Systems / Society

Notes to future you (or future adopters) on how to tune this stack.

## From brief to course

Original brief phrases:

- “Speakers move people and politics; let's tune both.”
- Trace sound system roots.
- Mix in community.
- Design small PA rigs.
- Document gatherings.
- 2-week starter arc:
  - Week 1: history and listening party.
  - Week 2: build tiny system.
- Assessment mix: participation, mix demo, reflection.
- Access notes: provide ear protection and remote mixes.
- Artifacts: syllabus, final project, process log.

The syllabus and assignments here are built directly from that spine.

## Pacing and load

- Written as a 14-week, 3-credit class with:
  - one 3-hour meeting per week, or
  - two 80–90 minute meetings.
- To compress:
  - merge portions of Projects 1 and 2, or
  - keep the Listening Log but reduce required entries.

## Assessment mix (minimal viable)

If you want a lighter structure closer to the original brief:

- Participation and presence — 25%
- Listening Log — 15%
- Tiny System Design & Demo (Project 2) — 25%
- Final Project (Community Mix & Documentation) — 35%

Project 1 can be folded into in-class activities and short written assignments rather than a full paper.

## Access and safety practices

- Always have:
  - foam earplugs or comparable ear protection in the room,
  - a plan for maximum volume levels,
  - a quick way to mute the system if feedback or harsh levels hit.
- Normalize:
  - students asking for volume reductions,
  - students stepping out briefly if overwhelmed,
  - remote/recorded alternatives to in-person high-volume events.

### Remote mixes and “quiet routes”

Where students cannot attend loud or crowded spaces:

- Allow:
  - headphone-level mixes in controlled environments,
  - “silent disco”–style experiments on campus (if gear allows),
  - fully remote events with shared playlists and chat.

Encourage students to design “quiet routes” through their projects: ways the work can be experienced at lower levels or via transcripts, captions, or descriptions.

## Rubrics (sketch-style)

Each assignment file includes rubric sketches; you can formalize these into point-based rubrics if your institution requires it. A common breakdown:

- Conceptual clarity — 25%
- Systems thinking (technical + social) — 25%
- Craft and execution — 25%
- Reflection and connection to readings — 25%

## Gear realities

You can run this course in several gear regimes:

1. **Classroom PA only**
   - Use the existing install as your primary case study.
   - Students design hypothetical rigs and test smaller signal chains.
2. **Small mobile rigs**
   - 1–2 powered speakers + small mixer.
   - Easy to move into different rooms; ideal for tiny system labs.
3. **Lab / studio**
   - Multiple monitor pairs, interfaces, headphones.
   - Students can work in smaller groups or solo on mixes.

The briefs assume you can at least:

- route audio from a laptop/phone into speakers,
- change basic gain and EQ,
- and control overall level safely.

## Community and ethics

Invite students to consider:

- Who is this course for, beyond the classroom?
- How might their skills feed into:
  - campus events,
  - community organizations,
  - mutual aid or advocacy work?

If you have strong local partnerships, Projects 2 and 3 can be tied directly to:

- a campus center,
- a neighborhood group,
- a venue, gallery, or radio station.

Where that’s not possible, keep events small and classroom-centered, but still anchored in real spaces and constraints.

## Logs as infrastructure

- **Listening Log** is student-facing and should feel low-stakes and reflective.
- **Process Log** is more craft-focused; point students to it whenever they say “I did a bunch of work but I don’t know how to show it.”

You can optionally ask students to submit:

- a midterm check-in on both logs, and
- a final curated selection tied to the final project and reflection.
