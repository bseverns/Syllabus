**Learning outcomes:**
- Recognize that signs, maps, and public messages are designed systems.
- Identify where a room, hallway, school, or public place is confusing or unwelcoming.
- Create clearer public-facing artifacts using words, symbols, color, and layout.
- Revise a design after watching another person try to use it.

## Why
This course would give the repo an early civic-media precursor. It teaches young learners that design is part of public life: a sign can guide you, lose you, calm you, rush you, or leave you out. That makes it a beautiful feeder for later work in imaging, interface thinking, and civic media.

## Core idea
Students become designers of helpful public messages.

They study:
- hallway signs
- classroom labels
- library maps
- menus or schedules
- room instructions
- neighborhood flyers
- park rules or school notices

Then they redesign them for real people.

## Suggested arc
### Phase 1 - Notice the public voice
- what do signs and maps ask us to do?
- where do adults assume we already know too much?
- observation walks inside the school or nearby public space

### Phase 2 - Clarity, symbols, and layout
- arrows, icons, labels, headings, color
- what makes a message easy to read quickly?
- compare “too much” vs “just enough”

### Phase 3 - Helpful media prototypes
- redesign a sign, map, label system, or guide
- test with classmates or another small audience
- revise based on confusion points

### Phase 4 - Public kindness by design
- final project improves a real place or routine
- could be hallway wayfinding, classroom labeling, lunch flow, library guide, or neighborhood notice board
- end with a demo / walk-through, not just wall display

## Assessment mix
- observation notes
- sign and map critiques
- small redesign exercises
- peer testing records
- final public-facing helpful media prototype

## Materials / access notes
Low-cost core:
- clipboards
- pencils, markers, colored paper
- tape, cardboard
- printer access if possible
- cameras or tablets for observation photos

Optional upgrades:
- laminated signage
- QR codes
- simple digital maps
- shared slides or web pages

## Artifacts
- public observation notebook
- redesigned sign or label set
- one map or guide prototype
- user test notes
- final “helpful media” intervention

## Teacher notes
This course should stay concrete and generous. Avoid turning it into a civics lecture. Let students start from actual friction: “I got lost,” “I could not tell what to do,” “this sign talks weird,” “this place does not explain itself.”
