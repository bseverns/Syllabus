# DIY Instrument Lab (College) – Instructor Notes

## Positioning the course

This class lives at the intersection of:

- Sound art / experimental music  
- Sculpture / object making  
- Physical computing / electronics  
- Media and cultural theory

You can tilt it more toward any one of these depending on your program. Make that tilt explicit in Week 1 so students know whether to expect more sawdust, more solder smoke, more Max patches, or some balance of all three.

## Tech tiers

Tune the technical stack to your local realities.

- **Tier 0 – No-solder / low-voltage only**  
  - Breadboards, crocodile clips, battery power, “dead bug” prototyping  
  - Contact mics, passive mixers, feedback networks, acoustic experiments

- **Tier 1 – Basic soldering and simple ICs**  
  - NE555 oscillators, simple op-amp stages, passive filters  
  - Simple distortion and feedback instruments, “crackle boxes”

- **Tier 2 – Microcontrollers and MIDI / USB**  
  - Arduino/micro:bit/Teensy boards reading controls and sensors  
  - MIDI-over-USB to DAWs or softsynths, basic serial protocols

You do not need to use all tiers in one run of the course. It is perfectly acceptable to run a Tier 0–1 version with only a “glimpse” of Tier 2 via demos.

## Safety

Safety is non-negotiable and should be part of the aesthetic conversation, not an afterthought.

- **Electrical safety**  
  - Stick to low-voltage systems (e.g., 9–12 V DC) unless you have formal training and supervision for mains-level work.  
  - Use proper power supplies, protect against shorts, and check polarities before powering up.  
  - Have a clear policy for what *cannot* be plugged into house mains.

- **Hearing safety**  
  - Sound instruments can get loud quickly. Normalize earplugs, encourage lower monitoring levels, and take listening breaks.  
  - For any public showing, do at least one run-through at full-ish level to check it is tolerable and safe.

- **Physical safety**  
  - Soldering irons, saws, drills, hot glue, and sharp metal all demand training and supervision.  
  - Build in time for tool demos, practice joints, and safe clean-up.

## Access & embodiment

- Assume a diversity of bodies and neurotypes in the room.  
- Offer options for instruments that do not require standing, rapid motion, or fine motor control.  
- Encourage students to design with multiple possible players in mind.  
- Be mindful of strobe-like lights, high-frequency feedback, or other triggers that might be uncomfortable or unsafe.

Encourage students to treat access constraints as design prompts: *who can play this, how, and what would it mean to widen or narrow that group?*

## Budget & logistics

- Clarify early who buys what: does the department supply core components? Are there lab fees?  
- Maintain a shared parts bin of resistors, caps, transistors, perfboard, etc.  
- Have a labeled “graveyard” of failed builds students can scavenge from (while learning what *not* to do).

## Contingency planning

Instruments will break; that is part of the pedagogy.

- Normalize failure and repair as part of the work, not as shame.  
- Have some “emergency” instruments or controllers ready so performance night does not fall apart when a single board dies.  
- Encourage groups to have at least one backup plan for showcasing their idea (video demo, simulation, partial system).

## Archiving

Decide where the instruments and their documentation will live after the course:

- Retained by students, with department archiving only docs  
- Donated to a department instrument library  
- Exhibited temporarily, then disassembled for parts

Make this clear in Week 10–11 so students design with the eventual fate of the object in mind.
