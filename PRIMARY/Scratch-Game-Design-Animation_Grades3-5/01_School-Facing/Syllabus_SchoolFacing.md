# Scratch Game Design + Cutscenes Lab (Grades 3–5) — School-Facing Syllabus

**Grade level:** 3–5  
**Meeting time:** 1 hour/week for 12 weeks  
**Class size:** ~18 students  
**Platform:** Scratch (MIT) — scratch.mit.edu  
**Core promise:** Students build a playable game with **multiple levels** and **cut-scene animations**.

---

## Course overview
This course introduces students to game design and animation using Scratch, a block-based programming environment created by MIT.
Students will:
- design characters and worlds (visual art + story)
- build game rules (logic + debugging)
- animate cutscenes that connect levels (media making + sequencing)
- practice playtesting and revision (feedback + iteration)

The course emphasizes creativity, clarity, and persistence. Students learn to “think like designers,” making choices about how players move, what challenges mean, and how to communicate instructions.

Optional hardware on-ramp (if kits are available): students begin by building a simple **motion controller** with GPIO (Piper StoryMode) to learn how inputs become actions.


---

## Studio culture + story content
We treat games as *tiny worlds with rules*—and we help students notice what their worlds communicate. Through discussion, playtesting, and revision, students practice making work that is imaginative, respectful, and age-appropriate.

In kid-friendly language, we may discuss:
- **Character roles and fairness** (who gets power, who gets help, who gets heard)
- **Representation and stereotypes** (how we draw/design people and communities with care)
- **Conflict without cruelty** (puzzles, races, chase-and-escape, protect-and-rescue, “tag” mechanics)
- **Feedback as kindness** (“Two Stars + One Wish” playtesting norms)

**Content boundaries:** Projects may not include hate symbols, targeted bullying, or graphic content. We steer away from realistic weapons and encourage students to redesign intense ideas into safer, more creative mechanics and stories.

---

## Learning goals
By the end of the course, students will be able to:
- use events, loops, conditionals, and variables in Scratch
- create a game with **two or more levels** (with transitions)
- use **broadcast messages** to control cutscenes and scene changes
- animate characters using costumes, timing, and simple effects
- playtest projects and fix common bugs (reset logic, collision issues, score/lives errors)

---

## Weekly arc (high-level)
**Weeks 1–4:** Scratch basics + animation + score/lives  
**Weeks 5–7:** Level 1 design + clean transitions + cutscenes  
**Weeks 8–10:** Level 2 + enemies + polish (instructions, win/lose screens)  
**Weeks 11–12:** Debug + showcase

---

## Class format (what families / school can expect)
Each class follows a predictable structure:
1. **Short demo** (7 minutes or less)
2. **Build sprint** (hands-on creation with teacher support)
3. **Playtest rotation** (students test each other’s games)
4. **Share + save** (celebrate progress and document next steps)

Students will be encouraged to collaborate, but each student should leave with a complete project they can continue at home.

---

## Materials and technology
- Device with internet access that runs Scratch
- Mouse recommended for younger users
- Headphones optional
- Scratch account (or a class studio if accounts are managed centrally)

No prior coding experience is required.

---

## Participation and expectations
Scratch is most rewarding when students:
- try small changes, test, and keep iterating
- ask peers for help using respectful “coach language”
- save work often and name projects clearly
- treat playtesting as kindness: “help the game improve”

---

## Accessibility and differentiation
The course is designed so students can succeed at multiple levels:
- templates and “block recipes” for students who need more structure
- extension challenges for advanced students (clones, boss phases, additional cutscenes)

---

## Assessment (informal, growth-focused)
Assessment is based on:
- completing weekly milestones
- participating in playtests and revisions
- producing a final game that meets the capstone requirements

We prioritize creativity and perseverance over perfection.

---

## Showcase
In Week 12, students participate in a mini “game festival” and share:
- how their game works (controls + goal)
- their favorite level or cutscene
- one challenge they overcame
